Before it’s called perimenopause, or anxiety.

Fatigue, brain fog, joint pain or palpitations in midlife are often given a perimenopause or anxiety explanation at the first visit. Before accepting one, these ten checks should have been considered. Print it, tick what’s been done, and ask about the rest.

NICE guideline NG23 lets clinicians diagnose perimenopause in women over 45 from symptoms alone, without labs. This page is what should be weighed before that label settles: ask whether each was considered, not for the panel.

Usually part of a first workup

Standard, inexpensive checks that a fatigue or multi-symptom workup commonly includes. The ask: “were these considered?”

  • Thyroid disease and menopause share symptoms almost one for one: cycle changes, mood, sweats, sleep, hair. European menopause guidance treats the thyroid as part of telling them apart; ask whether free T4 and antibodies were included, not just TSH.

    Source: EMAS position statement, Maturitas 2024

  • Low iron stores can drive fatigue, brain fog and palpitations before anemia shows up, and a normal CBC doesn’t rule them out. Ask for the ferritin number, not just “normal.”

    Source: AAFP, Fatigue in Adults, 2023

  • The basic screen for anemia and other blood conditions that commonly present as exhaustion; a standard anchor of a first fatigue workup.

    Source: AAFP, Fatigue in Adults, 2023

  • Kidneys, liver, glucose and electrolytes in one draw: the other standard anchor of the initial workup.

    Source: AAFP, Fatigue in Adults, 2023

  • Deficiencies in either are common, cheap to check, and can drive fatigue, aches, low mood and cognitive complaints; clinicians evaluating unexplained fatigue often include them early.

    Source: CDC, Evaluation of ME/CFS

  • Persistent joint pain or symptoms across systems make an inflammation marker worth asking about; a raised result is a question to pursue with your clinician, not an answer by itself.

    Source: AAFP, Fatigue in Adults, 2023

  • Palpitations and fatigue in midlife women deserve a formally calculated risk score (cholesterol + blood pressure + the estimate itself) rather than reassurance by age or sex.

    Source: ACC/AHA primary-prevention guideline, 2019

When symptoms point there

Not screening tests. They earn their place when the picture fits. The ask: “do my symptoms make this one worth considering?”

  • A first screen for autoimmune disease when symptoms span systems: joints, skin, dryness, fevers. It’s symptom-triggered for a reason: roughly a third of healthy people test weakly positive (31.7% at 1:40; 13.3% at 1:80), so without a fitting picture it creates more confusion than answers.

    Source: Tan et al., Arthritis & Rheumatism 1997;40(9):1601–11

  • Heart rate and blood pressure lying down and then standing, over ten minutes, in the office. For when dizziness, a racing heart or worse-when-upright symptoms are part of the story.

    Source: CDC, Evaluation of ME/CFS

  • When the trouble with focus has been there for life and midlife made it unmanageable. Women are identified years later than men; a validated screener is a short questionnaire, not a guess.

    Source: Skoglund et al., J Child Psychol Psychiatry 2024

This is not an order sheet. Broad lab panels are low-yield: testing everyone for everything mostly finds noise, which is why family-medicine guidance keeps the first panel small. The ask is “was this considered?”, and “not needed, because…” is a perfectly good answer. Which of these fit your picture is your clinician’s call.

Discuss with your clinician. This page is general information, not medical advice for you, and it cannot say which of these apply to you. Bring it to the visit and weigh it together.
