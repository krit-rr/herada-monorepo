The Learning Library

The Women’s Health Bible, rebuilt as a plain-language message and micro-lesson library: what to expect from your body, and what care you are entitled to, mapped to the measures health plans are already held to.

What it is

Short messages and five-minute lessons, in English and Spanish, written at about a sixth-grade reading level. Each one is tied to a quality measure that plans and Medicaid programs already report on: cervical and breast cancer screening, prenatal and postpartum visits, postpartum depression screening, chlamydia screening, and bone care after a fracture. The library is licensed without any personal health information in it, because it never contains any.

Every message ends the same way: what to expect, and what to ask. Never a diagnosis.

Who it’s for

Women first. A Pap test is covered, free and routine, and most women don’t know. Perimenopause arrives unannounced. Hormone therapy is covered on Medicaid, and many prescribers believe it isn’t. The library fills those gaps before the visit.

And the plans, clinics and schools that serve them. The same messages run inside the outreach a plan already does, and are measured against a comparison group, so a screening that happens because of them shows up in the numbers.

Two problems, one evidence base

Problem 1 is preparation

Women aren’t taught what to expect from their bodies or what care they are entitled to, so they don’t ask for it. The Learning Library is the answer to that, and the Bible, the Rule-Out Checklist and the Ledger are its working parts.

Problem 2 is training

Clinicians aren’t taught how conditions present in women, so they reach for the nearest label. The Diagnostic Detour is the evidence; How It Presents in Women, a point-of-care micro-learning course for primary care, nurse practitioners and PAs, is the response. It starts once its clinical co-author is named, and it will be jointly provided with an accredited partner.

Also coming

The Misrouting Registry

A consented, research-only survey of how midlife women get routed between specialties before the right diagnosis. The published evidence for that detour is thin and scattered; the Registry is how we add to it, with an academic partner named here before it opens.

What’s asked. The visits, in order: who you saw, what they said, what was tested, what was tried. The time from first symptom to the diagnosis that held. Age band, state, insurance type. Nothing that names you, your clinicians or your clinic.

The verification filter. Everyone can submit; only journeys that end in a clinician-confirmed diagnosis are counted in the published analysis, so the Registry measures the detour to a diagnosis, not the detour to a belief.

Consent, in plain words.

  • You choose what to share, and you can stop at any point.
  • Your answers are used for research and publication only, in aggregate, with no way back to you.
  • The survey runs on a separate, consented form service, not on this site, and the terms you agree to are shown there before the first question.
  • You can ask for your entry to be removed before publication.

What happens to the results. They are published, here and with the academic partner. They are never sold, licensed or shared with advertisers, insurers or employers.
